Two Networks, One User

Growing up between China's internet and the open web, inside a culture of optimization, and what that taught me about who technology is built for.

I grew up in Haidian, the district of Beijing where China’s technology companies cluster and where parents are famous for raising high-achieving students. I now study data science in New York, about 11,000 kilometers away. The larger distance I travelled, though, was between two internets. In Beijing, my life operated on WeChat, Alipay, and Bilibili, while Google, YouTube, and Instagram were blocked. In New York, those blocked services are the default, and the apps I grew up using are now the ways I connect back home.

Looking back, one idea that connects my family, my city, my field, and the technologies that changed me is optimization. I was raised to treat life as something to improve through effort and measurement, and data science, similarly, measures numbers and optimizes decisions. Growing up between two networks, inside a culture of optimization, has taught me that technology is not neutral. Every platform is built by someone, governed by someone, and optimized for something.

01

Identity and Technology

There are four identities that shape me and carry me through the world. I am Chinese, and more specifically a Beijinger from Haidian. Haidian is home to Zhongguancun, often called China’s Silicon Valley, and to many highly educated families, known as 高知家庭 (gāozhī jiātíng), who push their children hard in school and measure success in grades and rankings. At Harrow International School Beijing, I was taught in an English-language British system, so I grew up bilingual, every day shifting between Chinese family life and a British curriculum. I am now an international student in the United States, a newcomer on the network that used to be blocked. Finally, I am a builder. As Head Boy from 2023 to 2025, I coordinated more than 40 student leaders, and I founded the Harrow Art Society venture, which turned student artwork into hoodies and tote bags for a social cause. Today, technology is my main building material.

Growing up in Haidian shaped my relationship with technology in two ways. First, technology was the local industry and the local ambition. The companies behind my everyday apps had offices nearby, and a tech career was presented to me as the “right track” in life. Second, daily life in Beijing ran through super-apps. By the time I was a teenager, WeChat handled my messages, payments, school group chats, and even official notices, and a QR code paid for almost everything, significantly different from the segmented Western app system.

The Class 4 idea of “intelligence in the channel” describes how digital technologies interact “intelligently” with the text, sound, and images they carry, as autocorrect does (Starks, 2026c). WeChat is a channel with intelligence reaching far beyond its name. This one app was my bank, post office, school notice board, and news feed, and it shaped what information reached me. In China, that channel also operates within limits set by the government. The Great Firewall blocks most major Western platforms, so my network had its own apps, rules, and versions of the news.

I experienced this boundary by crossing it. My parents were open-minded about the Western internet, and my father is a big fan of Western culture, so using a VPN to search Google or watch YouTube lectures for school was normal at home. Still, my access to information depended on whether that connection could last. Bandwidth, as Class 4 framed it, is the width of a pipe that limits how much data can flow (Starks, 2026c), and behind the firewall, my pipe to the Western internet was narrow and unreliable (see Figure 1). As a new international student in New York, I found the problem reversed. My phone couldn’t pay for anything until I set up a U.S. phone number, a bank account, and new payment apps, so the network that was the default for everyone here locked me out for a short time. Today I live on both networks at once, with family in WeChat groups and NYU friends on iMessage and Instagram.

Reading both networks in two languages makes me see every feed as carefully curated. One event can have different ways of portraying it for different goals. My identities also make me aware of my access. My school and family gave me good devices, English-language materials, and a way around the firewall, which many students in China do not have. I benefited from those technologies perhaps because I was placed in an environment where I could access them.

02

Education, Profession, and Technology

At NYU, I study data science in the College of Arts and Science and plan to minor in computer science. My interest began with reading biographies of Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, and Sam Altman; Max Tegmark’s Life 3.0; and the story of Jim Simons, the mathematician who founded the quantitative hedge fund Renaissance Technologies. I wanted to understand how raw data becomes a large language model that can answer such complex questions. My home pushed in the same direction. My father ran a technology retail business before becoming an investor who trades U.S., Chinese, and Hong Kong stocks and options and invests in private companies. My mother is starting a small quantitative investment firm. My goal is a career in quantitative research, with building AI products as a second interest.

I have a considerable amount of exposure to technology training in both NYU courses and outside of them. NYU courses in statistics and experimental physics taught me that every measurement carries error, and courses in Python and linear algebra gave me the tools to work with data. As a research intern, I used a gravity model and multivariate regression to study how commodity prices relate to Peru’s trade flows. In research on Chinese A-share opening auctions, I used Python, SQL, and ClickHouse to build and backtest trading signals. I have also completed IBM coursework on transformers and machine learning; I built Lumi, an app that uses a large language model to turn plain-language tasks into deadlines and priorities.

The dominant worldview of data science is that what can be measured can be optimized. The field turns the world into data, picks a number such as accuracy, profit, or clicks, and improves the model until that number rises and is ultimately maximized. This worldview shapes how I see technology as an optimization machine and makes me ask which numbers it is using and how it is optimized. It also makes me optimistic about the future of AI, which shows how far data and optimization can go.

My own research showed me the limits of this worldview. One of my backtests produced a strategy that looked profitable on historical data. The backtest ignored two frictions: the time lag between a signal appearing and an order reaching the market, and whether an order would actually be filled at that price. A result like that can look excellent on screen but fail in a live market, because the numbers cannot fully predict how the real world acts (see Figure 2). Class 3 showed that AI has a similar limit. It is excellent at pattern recognition, but it cannot fully understand common-sense reasoning about the physical world (Starks, 2026b). In both cases, a system optimizes what it can measure and misses what it cannot. This is why I am an enthusiast for technology, but a conditional one. Before I fully trust a system, including one I build, I want to know what it optimizes for and whose data built it.

03

Values and Technology

My inherited values come from my family and from Haidian. My parents often repeated 吃得苦中苦，方为人上人 (chī dé kǔ zhōng kǔ, fāng wéi rén shàng rén), which means that only by enduring the bitterest hardship can you rise above others. They taught me to work as hard as possible now, choose a “right track” such as technology or quantitative finance, and earn enough to pursue my dreams and live more freely later. They also taught me humility. These values shaped my early use of technology. I got my first phone in middle school, and I had to hand over all my devices at night; my parents also discouraged gaming, although I loved Honor of Kings and the Chinese version of PUBG. At the same time, my father encouraged me to admire tech founders like Elon Musk. After many years, I still admire what those founders built, but I now read their stories as accounts of what one person chose to optimize, not as templates to copy (see Figure 3).

Since lockdown and my move to New York, these values have changed in three ways, and each one shapes how I use technology. First, hard work is now my choice rather than my parents’ rule. During lockdown, the discipline they once enforced by collecting my devices at night became self-imposed (see Turning Point 1). Though I am still on the track my parents pointed to, I no longer believe it is the only legitimate path. In the United States, I have seen that people have different talents and can succeed in so many different areas. I chose quantitative finance and AI because they fascinate me, not because they are ranked the highest as a career.

Second, I have stopped waiting to be “qualified.” The model I grew up with says to hide your dreams until you have earned the right to pursue them. Technology lowered the cost of starting. With AI as a tutor, I built Lumi as a student instead of waiting for a degree, and I founded a six-member Financial Data Science Interest Group in my first semester. I treat technology as a way to start building now rather than later, when I become qualified.

Third, my humility now meets a different culture. In New York, the most expressive and confident people often win, whether in class discussion, in clubs, or at networking events where students must present themselves. I am learning to speak up in class, including on Zoom, without giving up humility. Humility also guides how I use AI. Class 2 noted that AI is prone to “hallucinations” and struggles to recognize when it does not know something (Starks, 2026a). That is exactly the humility AI lacks, so I supply it by checking its answers. Reeves and Nass’s (1996, as cited in Starks, 2026b) media equation, which holds that people treat computers as social actors and are even polite to them, gives me a second reason for caution. When ChatGPT patiently explains something, it feels like a tutor, and that feeling can make me trust it more than I should.

04

Significant Turning Points

Turning point 1School Moves Onto a Screen

Between early 2020 and late 2022, COVID-19 closures moved my school in Beijing online several times, sometimes for months (see Figure 4). Class happened on Microsoft Teams, and the education was poor. Some students claimed their cameras and microphones were broken, and some stayed logged in long after the lesson had ended, a sign that no one was really there. We could not take proper tests. I paid little attention to the lessons either. Class 3 on working memory explains part of the problem. Working memory holds only about seven chunks of information at a time (Miller, 1956; Starks, 2026b), and switching between tasks has a cost (Hamilton, 2008). At home, the lesson, group chats, and other windows all competed for the narrow amount of information that we can process, so little of the lesson reached long-term memory.

Yet remote school became the most important turning point of my life. Without a commute, and with no one checking whether I was really listening, I had time and control. I was obese, lazy, and unmotivated, and I used that time to change. I learned bodyweight training, running, HIIT, and healthy eating from videos and posts on Bilibili and Xiaohongshu, and I trained with strict discipline. The discipline I learned from this passed on to other areas. I started studying seriously on my own, even when I ignored the lessons, and I became interested in reading. During lockdown, I stopped playing mobile games and gave that time to exercise, meditation, cooking, and my family.

This was an optimization, and it worked because I had a target. Like most recommendation feeds, Bilibili and Xiaohongshu are built to keep users watching, which serves the platform first. They helped me because I used them with a goal and searched for what I needed instead of letting the feed guide me.

Class 2 warned against framing technology as “either/or” instead of “both/and” (Starks, 2026a), and this turning point was both good and bad (see Figure 5). If Teams were taken away, school would simply have paused. I would have had even more free time and no pretend attendance, but I would have lost months of instruction and most contact with classmates. Removing Bilibili and Xiaohongshu reverses the trade-off. I would not have wasted time doomscrolling and could have used that time for sports outdoors and studying, but I would not have had as much guidance on my training, diet, and studying. Without these technologies and the lockdown itself, the change in me might never have happened, and I might still be the obese, lazy, and unmotivated child.

Turning point 2A Tutor That Never Sleeps

My second turning point was generative AI. I started using ChatGPT soon after it launched in late 2022. Getting an account from China was difficult, and I had to use a VPN to reach it, so the tool itself was a crossing between my two networks. Although I grew up bilingual, my written English grammar still needed work, so I first used ChatGPT to teach and correct my grammar, and then for everything I studied. Linear algebra is a good example. I am a visual learner, so I asked AI to explain the concepts and to render 3D planes that showed how each transformation moves and reshapes them. Seeing the planes change made abstract matrices concrete (see Figure 6). Over time, AI taught me enough to build Lumi, helped me do better academically, and gave me the courage to try things I had once avoided.

AI also exposed a contradiction. It creates something close to equal opportunity, because anyone with access gets a patient tutor. But access itself is unequal. I could reach ChatGPT because I had a VPN, a reliable connection, and enough English to use it. Language matters in a second way. Class 3 also asked whose memory large language models reflect and what goes into their datasets (Starks, 2026b). For GPT-3, an earlier OpenAI model whose training data was documented, 93% of the data by word count was English (Brown et al., 2020). That helps explain why ChatGPT was such a good English tutor, but it also means the model’s “memory” reflects English speakers far more than Chinese speakers like me.

One personal risk of using AI is the Google effect. When people expect information to stay available, they remember where to find it better than the information itself (Sparrow et al., 2011). AI goes further, because it produces step-by-step explanations as well as facts, so I could outsource understanding without really noticing. My rule is to let AI explain a method and then rework the problem by hand.

Without generative AI, I would have longer struggles with hard problems, which build deeper understanding, and less risk of trusting a fluent but wrong answer (see Figure 7). The drawbacks would be slower progress in my written English, no Lumi, and less of the confidence that now pushes me to try new things. I would still keep AI for one reason. I can recreate the benefits of struggle by choice, by closing the chat and working by hand. I cannot recreate a patient tutor at two in the morning.

05

Conclusion

Optimization is only as good as the target it serves, and who gets to choose it.

Haidian taught me to optimize, and data science gave me the tools to do it. My experiences suggest that optimization is powerful, but only as good as the target it serves. During lockdown, I set a clear goal and used Bilibili and Xiaohongshu to reach it, and it transformed my life. My backtest also pursued a clear goal, yet it ignored how real markets behave, so its result looked far better than reality. AI has accelerated my learning, but I could use it only because I had a VPN, a stable connection, and enough English. As Class 2 cautioned, technology is not an “either/or” but a “both/and” (Starks, 2026a), and my own experience confirms it. I am still on the track my parents pointed to, but I now choose my own targets, and I want to build systems that let others choose theirs.
